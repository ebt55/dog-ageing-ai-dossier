# Wolves vs dogs: did domestication change lifespan or ageing rate?
> Part of the dog-ageing research dossier · Written 2026-10-04 by Fable 5.1 subagent · Status: independently verified by Opus 5.5 on 2026-10-04 — see ../07_verification/breed_wolves_verification.md · Licence: [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

Tags: ✅ = confirmed in a primary source I opened (full text or the paper's own abstract); 🟡 = abstract/secondary only or press coverage; ❓ = unconfirmed, contested, or my inference.

## TL;DR

- Grey wolves (*Canis lupus*) are large canids (Yellowstone adults: males 45–59 kg, females 36–50 kg [2]; species-wide AnAge adult mass 26.6 kg [1]) that mature late (female sexual maturity ~669 days in AnAge [1]; mean age at first litter 2.7 y in Yellowstone [2]), breed once a year (inter-litter interval 365 d; litter ~4–6) [1][2] and live short lives in the wild (average 4–5 y inside Yellowstone, 2–3 y outside; oldest known Yellowstone wolf 12.5 y; wild record 13.7 y per AnAge) [1][2]. ✅ The NPS page and Holyan et al. also report wolf B-2 in Idaho, estimated at ~13.8 y at death (cementum annuli suggested 11 y), so the "wild record" is ~13.7–13.8 y and depends on age-estimation method [2][35]. [verifier-corrected]
- Wild wolf mortality is overwhelmingly extrinsic: in Yellowstone 42% intraspecific killing, 17% human, 8% interspecific, 5% malnutrition, 3% disease [2]; intraspecific aggression is the main density-dependent regulator of adult survival (280 collared wolves, 13 y) [3]; where hunting occurs, human-caused mortality dominates and annual survival falls from 0.78 to 0.57 (Minnesota) [6]. ✅
- Protected from those hazards, wolves live long: maximum recorded longevity 20.6 y (wild-born male that died in captivity) [1]; typical captive lifespans quoted as ~9–16 y [12]. 🟡 ❓ The review attributes the 9–16 y figure to Mech 1988, which is a note on *wild* wolf longevity, so its provenance for captivity is doubtful. The same review cites Lawler et al. 2016, where sanctuary wolves died at 5–19 y [12]. [verifier-corrected] That is as long as or longer than any large dog breed (large/giant purebred life expectancy 11.5/9.5 y in US clinical data [31]). ✅ So a ~40–60 kg canid can live ≥15–20 years; large-breed brevity is not an inevitable consequence of canid size. ❓ (inference)
- **No rigorous, size-matched comparison of ageing rate between dogs and wolves exists.** The only direct molecular comparison (Thompson et al. 2017; 46 dogs, 62 Yellowstone wolves, blood RRBS) built a joint 115-CpG clock but found almost no global correlation in age-related methylation between species (r = 0.07) and did not compare ageing rates [8]. ✅ Zoo-based life-table comparisons (Tidière 2016; >50 species, 84% live longer in zoos) do **not** include *Canis lupus*. The supplementary species table (61 species) lists the canids *Lycaon pictus*, *Nyctereutes procyonoides*, *Urocyon cinereoargenteus* and *Vulpes vulpes*, but no grey wolf [7]. ✅ [verifier-corrected: resolved from 🟡]
- Blood oxidative-stress markers do diverge: lipid damage rises with age in domestic dogs, whereas total antioxidant capacity and glutathione peroxidase rise with age in wild canids; the authors suggest artificial selection may have reduced antioxidant capacity in dogs [11]. ✅ (abstract)
- Domestication genetics with plausible physiological/ageing relevance: 36 selected regions, 19 brain-related and 10 in starch/fat metabolism (incl. *AMY2B* copy-number gain) [13]; structural variants in *GTF2I/GTF2IRD1* (Williams–Beuren locus) underlying hypersociability [16]; ~24% smaller brains than size-matched wolves [17] (not an unambiguous "evolutionary singularity" among 25 canids, though the authors note the result is sensitive to assumed ancestral trait values [18]) [verifier-corrected]; dogs are *not* paedomorphic wolves in skull shape [19]; loss of seasonality and increased fecundity in free-living dogs [20]. ✅
- Wolves carry the derived "large" allele at the *IGF1-AS* body-size variant; the "small" allele is ancestral and nearly vanished in Pleistocene wolves before resurging in small dogs [32]. ✅ Wolves hold ~14% more *structural variants* (indels >50 bp) per genome than breed dogs (Dog10K) [21]. ✅ [verifier-corrected: the original said "variants"]
- Genomic resources are mature: Dog10K (1,987 canids at 20×, incl. 63 wolves; >48 M variants) [21], a chromosome-level Greenland wolf reference (mCanLor1.2, 98.91% in 40 chromosomes) [23], a Greenland-wolf-outgroup variant map [24], and Zoonomia's 240-mammal alignment with 682 dog/wolf genomes [22]. ✅/🟡
- Germline engineering of *Canis lupus* is demonstrably feasible: Colossal Biosciences produced three living grey wolves (born 1 Oct 2024 and 30 Jan 2025) carrying 20 edits in 14 genes (15 dire-wolf-derived variants + 5 coat-colour edits incl. loss-of-function in *MC1R* and *MFSD12*) via CRISPR in blood-derived endothelial progenitor cells, SCNT into dog oocytes and domestic-dog surrogates [27]. 🟡 TIME confirms 20 edits in 14 genes, EPCs, and the birth dates; CNN confirms donor eggs from domestic dogs [34]. No peer-reviewed methods paper on the animals was found. [verifier note] The "dire wolf" branding is rejected by the IUCN SSC Canid Specialist Group and most specialists [27][28]. The ancestry study is now peer-reviewed (Gedman et al., *Cell Genomics* 6(8):101306, 27 Jul 2026) and reports dire wolves diverged ~4.5 Mya (95% CI 2.89–5.84), with ~39% ancestry from a lineage grouping with South American canids [26]. ✅ This revises Perri et al. 2021's 5.7 Mya split. Both papers agree dire wolves were a distinct lineage, and the new paper says ancient dual ancestry explains the gene-tree discordance Perri observed (Perri is a co-author) [25][26]. [verifier-corrected] Earlier, Zou et al. 2015 made MSTN-knockout beagles by zygote injection (35 zygotes → 27 pups → 2 edited; germline-transmissible) [30]. ✅

## 2. What kills wild wolves

- **Yellowstone (protected)**: adult mortality inside the park — intraspecific 42%, natural-unknown 15%, interspecific 8%, malnutrition 5%, other 4%, disease 3%, human 17% [2]. ✅
- **Cubaynes et al. 2014** (280 collared wolves, 13 years): in prey-rich northern Yellowstone, adult survival is regulated by density-dependent intraspecific aggression independent of prey; survival declined over the study; yearlings and adults had similar survival but wolves >6 y had lower survival (i.e., actuarial senescence is detectable even in the wild) [3]. ✅ (abstract)
- **Denali (Borg et al. 2015)**: 26-year dataset, 387 radio-collared wolves; breeder loss preceded 77% of 53 pack dissolutions; harvest of breeders during the breeding season raised dissolution risk, but population growth was buffered by compensatory mechanisms [4]. ✅ (abstract)
- **Isle Royale (Robinson et al. 2019)**: whole-genome sequencing of the isolated population shows severe inbreeding depression caused by homozygosity for strongly deleterious recessive alleles — the wolf analogue of the inbreeding burden carried by purebred dogs [5]. ✅ (abstract)
- **Hunted populations (Oliynyk 2023, N. Minnesota)**: 150 wolves, 2004–2019; human-caused mortality 10.0% before hunting vs 35.8% during/after; annual survival 0.783 → 0.566; elevated human-caused mortality persisted ≥5 y after hunting ended [6]. ✅
- Implication: wild wolf "lifespan" is almost entirely an extrinsic-mortality statistic and says nothing about intrinsic ageing rate; captive data are the relevant comparator for dogs, and those are thin.

## 3. Did domestication change lifespan or ageing rate?

### 3.1 Demographic comparisons
- Across mammals, zoo populations outlive wild ones in 84% of >50 species, with the largest benefit in fast-living species; the four metrics compared were longevity, baseline mortality, onset and rate of senescence [7]. ✅ (abstract) *Canis lupus* is **not** among the species (supplementary table checked). The only canids are African wild dog, raccoon dog, grey fox and red fox; longevity was higher in zoos for all carnivores analysed [7]. ✅ [verifier-corrected: resolved]
- Record wolf longevity (20.6 y) [1] exceeds median lifespans of all dog breeds and sits near the top of individual dog records; the typical captive range (9–16 y) [12] overlaps or exceeds large/giant breed life expectancy (9.5–11.5 y) [31]. 🟡❓ (The 9–16 y range has doubtful provenance; see §1. Note also that AnAge's 20.6 y is a single record, not a typical lifespan.) [verifier-corrected] There is no published Kaplan–Meier or Gompertz comparison of captive wolves vs size-matched dogs under comparable husbandry — the key missing experiment. ❓
- A 2025 review argues wolves are relatively protected from canine cognitive dysfunction-like decline, attributing dogs' vulnerability to inflammaging, oxidative stress, processed diets and periodontal disease, and notes it is "unusual for wild gray wolves to reach or surpass 10 years of age" [12]. 🟡 (narrative review; no primary cognitive-ageing data in wolves)

### 3.2 Epigenetic clocks
- **Thompson, vonHoldt, Horvath & Pellegrini 2017**: blood RRBS from 46 dogs and 62 Yellowstone wolves; clocks of 41 CpGs (dogs), 67 (wolves) and 115 (combined); combined clock LOO r ≈ 0.8, median absolute error 0.8 y; species-specific clocks weaker (dogs r = 0.65, wolves r = 0.54). Global correlation of age-related methylation changes between dogs and wolves was only r = 0.07, yet a shared clock could be built after removing species-specific sites. Age acceleration vs breed weight r = 0.25 (n.s., n = 46). Conservation with human age-related CpGs significant but small (r = 0.20). **The paper does not test whether dogs age epigenetically faster than wolves** [8]. ✅ Verifier note: the wolves were young (mean 2.7 y, median 2, range 0.5–8) compared with the dogs (mean 5, range 0.5–14), which the authors list as a possible reason for the low r = 0.07. This also limits any dog–wolf rate comparison from these data.
- **Horvath et al. 2022** built array-based clocks from 742 blood samples across 93 dog breeds (R = 0.97; median error <0.58 y) and dual dog–human clocks (R = 0.97), but included no wolves [9]. ✅
- **Lu et al. 2023 (Mammalian Methylation Consortium)** universal pan-mammalian clocks used 11,754 arrays from 185 species [10]; whether wolf samples were included and how wolf relative epigenetic age compares to dogs was not verified here. ❓ Verifier: the Lu 2023 main text (PMC10501909) never mentions wolves or *Canis lupus*. The species list is only in Supplementary Data 1.1–1.4, which was not checked (UNRESOLVED).
- Net: epigenetic tools exist to compare dog and wolf ageing rate on one platform (Horvath mammalian array, universal clocks), but the comparison has not been published to my knowledge. **This is a concrete, cheap research path**: profile captive wolves of known age across the lifespan on the mammalian array, compute relative epigenetic age, and compare to size-matched breeds.

### 3.3 Physiology
- **Jimenez & Downs 2020** (blood from domestic dogs and wild canids): lipid damage increases with age in dogs; total antioxidant capacity and GPx activity increase with age in wild canids; small-breed dogs have higher circulating lipid damage than large breeds despite longer lives; authors suggest artificial selection reduced antioxidant capacity in dogs [11]. ✅ (abstract) Cross-sectional; species, husbandry and diet confounded.
- Jimenez's fibroblast work (see breed_size_genetics_lifespan.md §3) has not yet been extended to wolf cells. ❓

### 3.4 Working conclusion
There is no evidence that domestication *per se* shortened intrinsic canid lifespan; the best-supported view is that (a) wild wolves die of extrinsic causes long before senescence dominates, (b) protected wolves live at least as long as large dogs, and (c) dog breed lifespan differences are driven by breed formation (size selection, inbreeding, disease alleles, conformation) rather than by domestication's shared genetic changes. ❓ (synthesis; see open questions)

## 4. Domestication genetics with physiological or ageing relevance

| Change | Evidence | Relevance to ageing |
|---|---|---|
| **Starch/fat metabolism (*AMY2B*, *MGAM*, *SGLT1* region)** | Axelsson et al. 2013: 3.8 M variants, 36 selected regions; 19 brain-related (8 in nervous-system development pathways), 10 in starch digestion/fat metabolism; functional support for increased starch digestion in dogs [13]. ✅ *AMY2B* copy number varies widely among dogs and correlates with amylase activity (Arendt 2014) and rose in ancient European dogs (Ollivier 2016) [14][15]. ✅ (abstracts; verifier-upgraded) | Diet-metabolism-insulin axis; dogs tolerate carbohydrate-rich diets, with unknown consequences for insulin/IGF signalling over the lifespan. ❓ |
| **Hypersociability (*GTF2I*, *GTF2IRD1*; Williams–Beuren locus, CFA6)** | vonHoldt et al. 2017: a 5-Mb region under positive selection; structural variants in *GTF2I/GTF2IRD1* contribute to extreme sociability; "directional selection may have targeted a unique set of linked behavioral genes" [16]. ✅ (abstract) | Behavioural; indirect (stress/HPA axis) relevance at most. ❓ |
| **Brain size reduction** | Dogs' absolute brain size is ~24% smaller than wolves' at equal body mass (159 breeds, 865 dogs) [17]. The ELTE/phys.org press release gives **48** wolf specimens, not 55, and ~131 vs ~100 cm³. The exact "24.32%", β = 0.745 and pMCMC = 0.652 were not checked (paper not open access) 🟡 [verifier-corrected]. Abstract-confirmed: breeds more distant from wolves have relatively larger brains, and relative brain size is unrelated to longevity, litter size or function [17] ✅. In phylogenetic context the dog's reduction is not an unambiguous evolutionary singularity among 25 canid species (residual −0.101; seven canids have larger absolute residuals). The authors note sensitivity to assumed ancestral values, and the raccoon dog is the clear outlier [18]. ✅ | Longevity–brain-size link absent within dogs [17]. |
| **Neoteny / paedomorphosis** | Drake 2011 (677 dogs/106 breeds vs 401 wolves, 3D morphometrics): "Dogs are not paedomorphic wolves"; no breed's skull resembles adult or juvenile wolves; dogs show de novo cranial flexion [19]. ✅ (abstract) | Undermines the popular "dogs are juvenilised wolves" framing; developmental-timing hypotheses about dog ageing should not assume neoteny. |
| **Reproduction** | Lord et al. 2013: free-living dogs show decreased seasonality, increased fecundity and reduced parental care vs other *Canis*, interpreted as adaptation to human settlements and waste [20]. ✅ Wolves: one litter/year, first litter ~2.7 y [1][2]. | Life-history theory predicts faster reproduction trades off against somatic maintenance; whether earlier/more frequent dog reproduction shifted intrinsic ageing is untested. ❓ |
| **Genetic diversity** | Dog10K: wolves have ~14% more *structural* variants than breed dogs (per-wolf 30,943 deletions / 16,071 insertions vs 26,960 / 14,034 per breed dog). 14.7% of biallelic autosomal SNV sites were present only in wolves, which made up 3% of samples [21]. ✅ [verifier-corrected: wording] | Breed bottlenecks + inbreeding (mean F ≈ 0.25 in breeds) raise deleterious-allele load; see breed file §4.1. |
| **Body-size regulation (*IGF1-AS*)** | Derived allele predominates in modern wolves and large breeds; ancestral small allele nearly vanished in Pleistocene wolves and resurged in small dogs [32]. ✅ | Wolves are genetically "large" at the major size locus yet long-lived in captivity — a natural control for the size–lifespan hypothesis. ❓ |

## 5. Dog–wolf genomic resources

- **Dog10K** (Meadows et al. 2023): 1,987 canids at 20× — 1,611 breed dogs (321 breeds), 309 village dogs, 63 wolves, 4 coyotes; >48 M variants (~34 M SNVs, 14.4 M indels, 144 k SVs) [21]. ✅ An integrated Dog10K multi-omics database was published in NAR 2025 (not opened). 🟡
- **Wolf reference genome**: mCanLor1.2 (Greenland wolf; Darwin Tree of Life/Wellcome Open Res 2021): 98.91% of assembly in 40 chromosomal pseudomolecules incl. X and Y; chosen as a close outgroup with minimal coyote admixture [23]. ✅ (secondary summary of the genome note) A 2024 bioRxiv "map of canine sequence variation relative to a Greenland wolf outgroup" polarises dog variants against this reference [24]. 🟡
- **Zoonomia** (Science 2023 special issue): 240-mammal alignment plus 682 dog and wolf genomes used for constraint analyses; also the Balto sled-dog genome [22]. 🟡
- Ancient canid genomes (Plassais 2022: 1,431 genomes incl. ancient wolves [32]; Perri 2021: five dire-wolf genomes [25]; Colossal/Gedman 2025 preprint, published 2026 in *Cell Genomics*: 3.4× and 12.8× dire-wolf genomes [26]). ✅

## 6. Genetic engineering of canids (proof of feasibility)

### 6.1 Colossal Biosciences' edited grey wolves (2024–2025)
- **Animals**: Romulus and Remus (males) born 1 Oct 2024; Khaleesi (female) born 30 Jan 2025; three living animals [27]. 🟡 (Wikipedia compilation of company statements and press; Colossal's own site confirms names and the 1 Oct 2024 date [29]; TIME confirms both birth dates and "20 edits in 14 genes" [34] [verifier-confirmed].)
- **Edits**: 20 edits across 14 genes; 15 based on dire-wolf genome variants, 5 for coat colour including loss-of-function changes in *MC1R* and *MFSD12* chosen to produce a pale coat without the deafness/blindness risks of the dire-wolf alleles [27]. 🟡
- **Method**: endothelial progenitor cells isolated from grey-wolf blood, multiplex CRISPR editing, somatic cell nuclear transfer into enucleated domestic-dog oocytes (dog donor eggs confirmed by CNN [34]), 45 engineered ova → 4 viable embryos, implanted in domestic-dog surrogates, delivered by planned caesarean [27]. 🟡 TIME confirms the 45 engineered ova. TIME describes the embryos going into two surrogate hound mixes (Romulus, Remus), with a later batch into a third surrogate (Khaleesi) and no miscarriages. The "4 viable embryos" figure appears only in Wikipedia and derivative sites. [verifier-corrected] Colossal's dire-wolf web page itself does not disclose edit counts or methods [29]. ✅
- **Genomic basis**: Gedman, Morrill Pirovich et al. (bioRxiv 9 Apr 2025; Cell Genomics 2026): dire-wolf genomes from a ~13,000-y-old Ohio tooth (3.4×) and a >72,000-y-old Idaho petrous bone (12.8×); dire wolves diverged ~4.5 Mya (95% CI 2.89–5.84), with ~39% ancestry from a lineage grouping with South American canids and ~61% from a sister lineage to dholes/coyotes/grey wolves; 99.94% sequence identity to grey wolf reference; *NCAPG* (adjacent to *LCORL*, a dog body-size locus) was the top gene under diversifying selection on the dire-wolf branch [26]. ✅ (preprint) The divergence time, 39%/61% ancestry and 99.94% identity are confirmed in the peer-reviewed version (*Cell Genomics* 2026, brief report). That version contains no *NCAPG*/selection analysis, so the *NCAPG* claim rests on the preprint only 🟡. It partly revises Perri et al. 2021 (five genomes; split ~5.7 Mya; no gene flow with North American grey wolves or coyotes) [25]. ✅ The new paper says dual ancestry explains the gene-tree discordance Perri observed; Perri, Larson and vonHoldt are co-authors. [verifier-corrected: "conflicts" softened; publication status updated]
- **Criticism**: IUCN SSC Canid Specialist Group: the animals are "neither dire wolves nor proxies" and the project "does not contribute to conservation" [27]; wolf biologists (Mech, Boitani), geneticists (Boyko) and palaeogeneticists (Rawlence, Austin) dispute the "de-extinction" label; press quotes estimate the animals are "optimistically 1/100,000th dire wolf" [27][34]. 🟡 Jensen-Seaman et al. 2026 (*Anat Rec*): "three pups possessing a small amount of dire wolf-like DNA born to a surrogate domestic dog"; discusses method, results and ethics [28]. ✅ (abstract)
- **Side project**: Colossal also announced four cloned red wolves in two litters: Hope, whose Karankawa name is "Neka Kayda", plus Blaze, Cinder and Ash. They were cloned from Gulf-coast "ghost wolf" canids carrying red-wolf alleles [27 press]. 🟡 [verifier: names confirmed in press]
- **Why it matters here**: regardless of the taxonomic argument, this is the first demonstration of **multiplex (≥20-site) germline editing in *Canis lupus* with live births**, via a blood-derived somatic cell line + SCNT in dog surrogates. The same pipeline could in principle test ageing-relevant edits (e.g., *IGF1-AS* allele swap, breed disease alleles, tumour-suppressor copy number) in canids, subject to severe welfare and ethical constraints.

### 6.2 MSTN-knockout beagles (Zou et al. 2015)
- Cas9 mRNA + *MSTN* sgRNA injected into beagle zygotes; 35 zygotes transferred to 10 recipients; 8 pregnancies, 27 puppies; 2 carried *MSTN* mutations — puppy #5 biallelic with an obvious double-muscled thigh phenotype by 4 months ("Tiangou"); puppy #11 mosaic (~0.5% wild-type) with no visible phenotype ("Hercules") but a single −4 bp mutant genotype in sperm, i.e., germline transmission [30]. ✅ (OUP full text)
- Shows zygote-injection editing works in dogs but with low efficiency (2/27) and mosaicism; the Colossal SCNT route gives pre-validated genotypes at the cost of cloning inefficiency (4 viable embryos from 45 ova) [27]. 🟡

## 7. Research paths suggested by the wolf comparison

1. **Size-matched captive wolf vs large-breed dog life tables** using Species360/ZIMS and sanctuary records — the missing demographic test of whether large dogs die young because they are large canids or because they are *breeds*. ❓
2. **Pan-mammalian methylation array on captive wolves** across ages (cheap; reagents and clocks exist [9][10]) to compare relative epigenetic ageing rate and size-dependence against dogs [8]. ❓
3. **Wolf-derived cells** (fibroblasts, iPSCs, or the endothelial progenitor lines Colossal already banks) as the "large canid, non-breed" control for the Jimenez-type glycolysis/oxidative stress assays [11]. ❓
4. **Polarising dog longevity GWAS/EWAS hits against the wolf allele** (Dog10K wolves [21], mCanLor1.2 [23]) to distinguish derived dog alleles (candidates for reversion) from ancestral states.
5. **Ancient-DNA time series** (Plassais 2022-style [32]) for GH/IGF-axis alleles to date when breed-specific growth alleles rose in frequency relative to lifespan-relevant selection.
